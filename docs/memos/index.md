## Purpose of the Memo Series

The investment memo series is designed to help you turn financial analysis into professional recommendations.

In the labs, you build models and interpret results. In the memos, you explain what those results mean for a decision-maker. A strong finance professional does not simply calculate numbers. A strong finance professional communicates a clear recommendation, supports it with evidence, acknowledges uncertainty, and explains why the decision matters.

The memo sequence also prepares you for the final capstone. Each memo becomes a building block for a larger investment recommendation.

<div class="callout-finance">
<strong>Professional insight:</strong> Financial analysis creates value only when it helps someone make a better decision.</div>

## Why Memos Matter

Investment memos are used in professional finance because decision-makers rarely want every detail of the model. They want a concise, evidence-based recommendation.

A good memo answers four questions:

1. **What is your recommendation?**
2. **What evidence supports that recommendation?**
3. **What assumptions or risks could change the conclusion?**
4. **What should the reader do next?**

The goal is not to write a long essay. The goal is to make a disciplined financial argument.

## Standard Memo Structure

Unless otherwise instructed, use the following structure for each memo.

### 1. Recommendation

State your recommendation clearly in the opening paragraph.

Examples:

- I would continue analyzing this company as a potential investment candidate.
- I would place this stock on a watchlist rather than recommend immediate purchase.
- I would not approve the project under the current assumptions.
- I would revise the financing plan before proceeding.

### 2. Key Evidence

Identify the most important financial evidence supporting your recommendation.

This may include:

- Financial ratios
- DuPont analysis
- Present value or future value estimates
- Bond value or yield evidence
- Stock valuation estimates
- Risk and beta analysis
- WACC
- NPV and IRR
- Working capital or leverage metrics

### 3. Interpretation

Explain what the evidence means.

Do not simply report numbers. A professional memo should explain why the numbers matter for the decision.

### 4. Risk or Limitation

Acknowledge at least one important limitation.

Examples:

- The analysis depends on the assumed growth rate.
- The recommendation is sensitive to the discount rate.
- The model does not include taxes, transaction costs, or strategic risks.
- Historical returns may not predict future returns.
- A financially strong company can still be overvalued.

### 5. Final Decision

Close by restating the recommendation and linking it to the evidence.

## Professional Writing Standards

Your memo should be:

- Clear
- Concise
- Evidence-based
- Professionally written
- Organized around a decision
- Honest about uncertainty
- Free of unsupported claims

A strong memo is not just technically correct. It is useful to the reader.

<strong>Memo standard:</strong> Lead with the decision. Support it with evidence. Explain uncertainty. End with a clear recommendation.</div>

## Evidence Expectations

A memo should use evidence from the relevant lab and module.

The evidence should be specific enough that the reader can understand the basis for the recommendation.

Weak evidence sounds like this:

> The company seems good and has strong numbers.

Stronger evidence sounds like this:

> The company appears financially healthy because profitability is strong, return on equity is supported mainly by profit margin rather than excessive leverage, and liquidity appears adequate based on the current and quick ratios.

The second version is stronger because it identifies the financial basis for the judgment.

The final capstone should not feel like starting from scratch. The memo series helps you build the capstone in stages.
